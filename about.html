About the whale map

Vessel strikes are one of the leading anthropogenic causes of large whale mortality. The Norwegian Sea, Barents Sea and surrounding waters host fin, blue, sperm, humpback and minke whales alongside some of Europe's busiest shipping lanes. This tool overlays cetacean observation density with gridded vessel density. Port and region scores use weighted observations and a speed factor; the shipping overlay is visual context and is not used numerically in those scores.

Why this exists

The Mesh Oslo "What else can you build" prompt highlighted whale incident tracking as an open problem. Hubocean.earth and the One Ocean Expedition show how rich open ocean data has become; this app uses a small, public slice of that for a single, pragmatic question: where do whales and ships overlap, and how does cutting vessel speed change strike lethality.

A sister project, Coexisting with Birds, answers the same question for wind farms and birdlife.

Conservation status

The map shows two parallel categorisations. The Norwegian Red List 2021 (Artsdatabanken) covers national populations; the global IUCN Red List covers the species worldwide. We display the stricter of the two, and call out the divergence where it matters (e.g. the fin whale is VU globally and LC nationally because the Norwegian feeding population is recovering).

Heatmap and points

The heatmap uses a 10K stratified sample from GBIF (taxonKey 733) covering the bounding box lat 56-82, lon -10 to 70 across all twelve months. Counts reflect where people record whales, not absolute abundance. Coastal and tourist viewpoints (Andoya, Lofoten, Tromso) are overrepresented. Points view shows individual records colored by species; click a point for details.

Per port strike risk

Each major port is colored from green (low) through yellow to red (very high) based on a composite weighted score of nearby observations.

Step 1. Find nearby observations
For each port, all cetacean observations within a 30 km radius are collected. Distance is computed using a flat earth approximation (sufficient at sub-100 km scales).
dx = (obs.lon - port.lon) × 111 × cos(port.lat) dy = (obs.lat - port.lat) × 111 distance = sqrt(dx² + dy²) // in km
Step 2. Weight each observation
Each observation starts with weight 1. Three multipliers are applied: red list weight, strike vulnerability and a relative speed factor.

Step 3. Normalise and color
The raw score is divided by 300 (author chosen display threshold, not a calibrated risk probability) and clamped to [0, 1]. Color interpolates green to red.
normalized = min(1, score / 300) Color mapping: 0.0 - 0.2 → green (low strike risk) 0.2 - 0.5 → yellow (moderate) 0.5 - 0.75 → orange (high) 0.75 - 1.0 → red (very high)

Vessel speed and lethality

The probability that a struck large whale dies or is severely injured rises sharply with vessel speed. The app uses the Vanderlaan and Taggart 2007 logistic Vanderlaan A.S.M. 2007 Vanderlaan A.S.M., Taggart C.T. (2007). Vessel collisions with whales: the probability of lethal injury based on vessel speed. Marine Mammal Science, 23(1), 144-156.

Logistic curve for P(lethal) vs vessel speed in knots. β0 = -4.89, β1 = 0.41. P=0.5 at v=11.8 kn.

doi: 10.1111/j.1748-7692.2006.00098.x

Open source :

P_lethal(v) = 1 / (1 + exp(-(β₀ + β₁ · v))) β₀ = -4.89, β₁ = 0.41 v in knots P=0.21 at v= 8.6 kn (NOAA slow zone target) P≈0.49 at v=11.8 kn P=0.79 at v=15.0 kn (typical merchant cruising) P→1.0 above ~18 kn

The 10 knot line is an illustrative comparison. With the coefficients used here, the conditional estimate is about 31% at 10 knots. This curve describes injury given a collision for large whales, not the probability of encountering or striking a whale.

Encounter rate (advanced)

For per cell mortality, Rockwood et al. 2017 ( Rockwood R.C. 2017 Rockwood R.C., Calambokidis J., Jahncke J. (2017). Encounter-theory mortality model. Per-cell expected strikes integrate whale density, ship density, vessel speed and lethality.

doi: 10.1371/journal.pone.0183052

Open source ) factor the rate of encounters out of the strike physics:

λ_e = 2 r_c (v_b + v_w) · I(v_b, v_w) / S r_c = (L_whale + W_ship) / 2 M = encounters · P_strike_depth · (1 - P_avoidance) · P_lethal(v_b)

I(v_b, v_w) is the Gerritsen-Strickler velocity correction term; for v_b ≫ v_w it approaches 1. P_strike_depth is the fraction of time the whale is in the hull-swept depth band (typically 0.4 to 0.6 from tag data). P_avoidance is the chance of last second evasion (0 by default, conservative).

Region density layer

When toggled on, each sea region is colored by observation count. Empty regions appear in grey, indicating low data confidence rather than absence of whales. With a 10K sample over four large IHO sea areas, expect at most a few thousand records per region.

Limitations

This is a screening tool, not an environmental impact assessment. Cetacean records reflect observer effort, not true density. EMODnet's vessel density grid is a 2017 to 2024 average. Norwegian-waters specific strike-risk modelling is sparse in the literature; we apply global physics and global species accounts. For real planning, use site specific surveys, Kystverket / BarentsWatch live AIS and modelled species distributions from Havforskningsinstituttet ( Havforskningsinstituttet (Institute of Marine Research) 2026 Havforskningsinstituttet (Institute of Marine Research) (2026). Saved sample and place names

The bundled GBIF sample was committed in April 2026. The original retrieval timestamp is unavailable. Its 9,996 observations span multiple years and use monthly quotas; month coverage is not seasonal abundance. Saving a browser snapshot does not refresh these observations.

Place names come from GeoNames, under CC BY 4.0. The local extract covers populated places in Norway. External OpenStreetMap and EMODnet tiles are not stored by the application snapshot.
